Case Study 01

Building the Experimentation Programme from Zero

There was no CRO function when I joined. No testing tool, no process, no internal case for one. This is how that became a fully owned, evidence-driven experimentation programme operating at scale.

The challenge

FXCM and subsequently Tradu were making product and acquisition decisions based on instinct, agency recommendations and historical assumption. Customer behaviour on key journeys, including the homepage, onboarding flow and landing pages, was not being measured in a way that connected to decisions.

The brief was not just to run tests. It was to build the capability: the tools, the process, the cross-functional buy-in, and the evidence quality standard that would make experimentation a reliable input into the product roadmap.

Programme Architecture
01 Data and behavioural audit. ContentSquare, GA4 and Mixpanel used to map friction across homepage, LP and onboarding journeys. Scroll maps, click heat maps and funnel drop-off rates identified where users were losing intent.
02 Qualitative discovery. In-depth interviews and usability sessions run to understand the why behind behavioural patterns. Hypothesis inputs gathered directly from users, not assumed from data alone.
03 Hypothesis workshops. Cross-functional sessions with product, design, engineering and marketing to prioritise hypotheses against effort, confidence and potential impact. Backlog managed in Jira throughout.
04 Deployment and monitoring. Tests built and deployed in Optimizely. Results monitored in ContentSquare, GA4 and Mixpanel with pre-agreed significance thresholds. No decisions made before statistical confidence was reached.
05 Ship or retest decision. Owned the final call on what went to production. Findings from every test fed back into the research backlog to generate the next wave of hypotheses.

Case Study 02

Research as the Conversion Intelligence Engine

Most CRO programmes fail because they run out of good hypotheses. The experimentation function at Tradu stayed productive because research and experimentation were designed as one continuous loop, not two separate workstreams.

◐
Signal

Behavioural Data Review

ContentSquare, GA4 and Mixpanel used to identify friction points, drop-off patterns and engagement anomalies. Data surfaced the where.

◑
Discovery

Qualitative Exploration

Usability testing and in-depth interviews conducted specifically to understand the why behind behavioural patterns the data alone could not explain.

◒
Strategy

Hypothesis Development

Cross-functional workshops to generate, challenge and prioritise hypotheses. Each test entered the backlog with a defined rationale, not just a creative preference.

◓
Learning

Results to Research

Every test result, whether it won, lost or was inconclusive, generated new research questions. The cycle was continuous and self-reinforcing.

What this meant in practice

The backlog was never empty because every experiment created new questions. When a homepage test increased scroll depth but did not move CTA conversion, the follow-up was not another button colour test. It was a qualitative study to understand what users were reading and why they were still not converting. That is what kept the programme generating commercial impact rather than running out of ideas.

Compliance as a design constraint

In regulated financial services, experimentation is not just a product decision. Every test involving customer data, messaging or journey design operated within GDPR-compliant consent processes, ethical research governance and internal compliance review. These were not bolt-on checks. They were built into the programme architecture from the start, anticipating Consumer Duty requirements before they became mandatory.

Case Study 03

Reaching the Audiences Standard Acquisition Research Missed

Conversion data tells you that users are dropping off. It does not tell you which users, or why. A targeted survey programme built to reach disengaged and hard-to-recruit segments changed what the product team understood about where acquisition was actually failing.

The Gap

Usability testing and standard recruitment methods consistently over-represented engaged, digitally confident users. The segments with the highest drop-off rates, users who had started onboarding but not completed it, and users who had visited but never converted, were not reachable through standard research recruitment. The product team was optimising for the users it could hear, not the ones it was losing.

The Approach

Designed a survey programme specifically architected to intercept disengaged segments at the right moment in the journey. Integrated surveys into email campaigns targeting users who had abandoned onboarding, and embedded rating scales into FAQ articles where users who had not converted were spending time. Segmented results by journey stage, platform and customer profile to surface distinct barriers that aggregate data had been masking.

Why it mattered for CRO

The findings changed what went into the experimentation backlog. Tests that had been prioritised based on the visible majority were deprioritised in favour of journey fixes that the hard-to-reach data showed were blocking a larger proportion of potential converters. The product roadmap shifted as a direct result.
